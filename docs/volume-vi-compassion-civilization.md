## Act XXI — The End of Extraction

### Chapter 101 — Human Worth Beyond Output

A person is not a balance sheet.

A society can count the work necessary to feed, shelter, heal, educate, and care for its people without assigning a numerical worth to the people themselves.

The Nevora proposal begins with a distinction: contribution is something a person may offer; dignity is not something a person must earn.

A child is not a worker in waiting. A person living with disability is not a failed productivity target. An exhausted caregiver does not owe another hour to prove belonging. A person who refuses a role is still a person.

What are you good at? What would you like to learn? What do you need? What can we do together?

These are invitations, never tests of eligibility for food, shelter, medicine, or respect. Skills change. Needs change. No individual owes the community a particular profession or a permanent identity.

A teammate society is one possible ethical horizon. Its practical expression must be voluntary, open to criticism, and compatible with life outside the community. It cannot demand ideological agreement or emotional intimacy.

The proposal is not that every person will always act generously. It is that our institutions can protect care even when generosity is absent.

A resource ledger may count kilograms, hours, and kilowatt-hours. It must never become a ledger of human worth.

**Boundary:** philosophical principle and design goal, not evidence that a community can supply unlimited necessities.

### Chapter 102 — The Difference Between Enough and More

An enterprise needs resources to function. It needs equipment, maintenance, skills, reserves, and the ability to respond when plans fail.

In a monetary setting it also needs financial sufficiency.

The question is not whether a surplus may exist. The question is what the surplus is for, who governs it, and whether the pursuit of more may override the dignity of those who make it possible.

A purpose-bound enterprise may choose a reserve target, a fair compensation policy, and a reinvestment plan. Once these needs are met, additional capacity could support lower prices, safer working conditions, shorter working time, public research, ecological repair, or other democratically authorized purposes.

These options involve tradeoffs. A reserve that is too small threatens continuity. A reserve that is never considered sufficient may become a justification for hoarding. A price below cost can transfer harm to workers or suppliers. A rule that rewards only visible output can conceal care work.

No single surplus percentage is proposed as universal. Any policy must be tested against costs, risk, member consent, external obligations, and the interests of affected nonmembers.

The longer design horizon is a voluntary society where access to ordinary goods and services can increasingly occur without personal monetary exchange. This does not imply money can be abolished immediately, or that resources cease to be scarce.

A transition must preserve lawful external trade, fair compensation, personal savings, and independent choices. It must not pressure anyone to surrender assets, income, or benefits.

**Boundary:** a proposed organizational model. Its legal, economic, and empirical claims require separate review.

### Chapter 103 — Compassion as an Institutional Constraint

Compassion is not a substitute for an operating budget.

Nor is it a decorative sentence in a charter that management can disregard when inconvenient.

In a proposed Nevora-aligned enterprise or resource commons, compassion becomes a set of enforceable design constraints: informed consent, a non-discriminatory needs floor within available capacity, independent review, accessible appeals, worker safety, privacy, and freedom of exit.

A human decision-maker must be accountable for allocation decisions. Computational systems may help compare inventories and schedules, but they may not decide whose life is worth more. A system must not use loyalty, participation, reputation, wealth, belief, or productivity as a condition for basic care.

Scarcity is a real possibility. When an essential supply falls short, the community should disclose aggregate shortfalls, consult affected people, seek replenishment, use published non-discriminatory allocation criteria, and permit timely review. Clinical allocation decisions belong to qualified professionals under applicable standards, not general-purpose software or a vote on someone's worth.

Care is not debt. A person who receives help does not become obligated to repay the helper with labor, allegiance, data, or affection.

A compassionate institution must also protect the people performing difficult work. It should first remove avoidable burdens, improve safety, offer training, and coordinate voluntary coverage. It must acknowledge unresolved shortages rather than compel unpaid work.

Governance requires checks on the people who claim to govern compassion itself. An independent appeal, transparent aggregate records, conflict-of-interest disclosure, rotating or replaceable authority, and a right to stop unsafe experiments are proposed safeguards.

A compassionate system is not defined by how warmly it describes itself. It is defined by what happens to a person who says no.

**Boundary:** philosophical and design proposal. The companion [coordination experiment](volume-vi-coordination-experiment.md) specifies a deliberately small, reversible test.
